Editorial Policy

This page explains how Morse Trainer plans educational content, reviews article quality, handles corrections, and keeps public learning material clearly separated from advertising decisions.

Last updated: July 21, 2026 · Public editorial standards for tutorial pages, public support content, and site corrections on mmccode.com.

Who maintains editorial review

Public tutorial, support, and policy pages are maintained under the Morse Trainer project rather than being left as one-time static uploads.

When content quality, factual clarity, or translation accuracy is challenged, the review path runs through the public contact channel and the relevant policy page before a page is kept live as-is.

1. Editorial purpose

Morse Trainer publishes practical educational content for people who want to understand Morse code, build listening skill, improve training habits, and connect drills with real learning use cases.

The goal is to make public content genuinely useful on its own, not to publish thin pages whose only purpose is search traffic or ad placement.

2. How content is created

Articles are planned around real learner questions, common training blockers, product features, and public-interest topics such as rhythm recognition, speed growth, copying practice, and amateur radio context.

Drafting tools, translation tools, structured notes, and product knowledge may all contribute to the writing process, but published pages are expected to be reviewed, edited, and aligned with the site’s learning goals before release.

3. Review standards

Before publication, content should be checked for clear structure, basic factual consistency, readable language, and direct usefulness to Morse learners. Pages should explain a method, a decision, or a learning problem in practical terms.

Content should avoid exaggerated claims, copied material presented as original work, misleading headlines, and filler text that does not help the reader make progress.

4. Updates and corrections

Tutorials may be updated when product features change, better explanations are available, broken links are found, or a reader points out a meaningful issue. Corrections should favor clarity over defensiveness.

Material changes should be reflected in the page’s Last updated label or structured dateModified field so readers can see when a major correction, rewrite, or scope change took place.

If you notice an error or unclear explanation, use the public Contact page to report it. For common product questions before reporting a content issue, readers should check the Help Center. The site also maintains related public pages such as About, Privacy Policy, and Terms of Use.

5. Advertising and editorial separation

Educational content decisions should not be based on whether a page is easy to monetize. Tutorial rankings, internal recommendations, and learning advice should be driven by user value, not by ad pressure.

Any advertising shown on the site should be labeled and placed in ways that do not hide training controls, distort tutorial intent, or imitate normal site navigation. See Advertising Disclosure for more detail.

6. Source and copyright standards

History, culture, and operating-context pages should be based on broadly available reference material, technical knowledge, or original educational explanation instead of copied passages presented as new writing.

The site should not publish full copyrighted transcripts from films, games, books, or paid training resources just to pad pages. When a public example is discussed, it should be summarized in original language, kept brief, and used for learning context rather than content scraping.

For the broader research process behind those choices, see Research & Sources.

How examples are chosen without padding content

Examples should be included because they clarify a learner problem, not because they make a page look longer. A good example shows why a method works, why a misconception fails, or how a historical case connects to practice. A weak example is decorative filler that repeats the heading without improving understanding.

When an example is too thin, too repetitive, or too dependent on copyrighted source material, it should be shortened, rewritten, or removed. The editorial goal is to keep public pages useful enough to stand on their own without pretending that extra paragraphs always mean extra value.

7. Multilingual and translation review

Morse Trainer publishes public content across multiple interface languages, so translation review matters. Important tutorial and support pages should be checked for broken placeholders, missing sections, mojibake, and terminology drift between English, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Spanish versions.

Translation tools may assist the drafting process, but final public wording should still be reviewed for readability, learning clarity, and whether the translated page still matches the original teaching purpose.

8. Reader trust signals

Morse Trainer aims to keep a transparent public footprint: a clear project description, visible contact information, policy pages, a tutorial directory, and content that matches what the site actually offers inside the product.

That trust layer matters both for real learners and for public review processes such as search-quality evaluation and advertising-platform assessment.

How pages are retired, merged, or rewritten

If a public page becomes redundant, misleading, outdated, or too thin to justify standing alone, it may be rewritten, merged into a stronger hub page, or removed in favor of a clearer replacement. That decision should be based on reader value and site clarity, not on preserving every old URL forever.

When possible, navigation links, directory references, and sitemap coverage should be updated so learners can still reach the best current version of the topic. This helps the site behave like a maintained educational project rather than a pile of abandoned drafts.
