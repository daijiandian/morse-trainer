Morse Code Glossary

This reference page explains the terms that appear most often when people begin Morse training, read practice guides, and move toward amateur radio operating language.

Last updated: July 18, 2026 · Public reference content maintained on mmccode.com as part of the Morse Trainer learning site.

What this glossary is for

Many beginners get slowed down not only by the sounds of Morse code, but also by the vocabulary around it. Training pages may mention Koch order, WPM, copying, QSO terms, paddles, or QRM before a new learner knows what those words actually mean.

This glossary gives short, practical explanations first. It is not meant to replace deeper tutorials. It is meant to make the rest of the site easier to understand and easier to use with confidence.

Beginner listening and training terms

CW

Short for continuous wave. In practice, many Morse learners use CW as a shorthand way to refer to Morse sending and receiving on radio.

WPM

Words per minute. This is the common speed label for Morse practice, but useful learning speed depends on both character speed and overall spacing.

Koch method

A training method that starts with a small character set and adds new characters only after recognition becomes stable.

Farnsworth spacing

A method that keeps character rhythm relatively fast while increasing the spaces between characters or words so beginners can process what they hear.

Copying

The act of hearing Morse and writing or typing what was received. Many learners treat copying as the bridge between single-character drills and real message work.

Head copy

Understanding Morse directly in your head without writing every character down first. This usually comes later, after rhythm recognition and basic copying become easier.

Character speed

The speed of each character itself, separate from the spaces around it. Two practices can share the same character speed while feeling very different overall.

Pattern recognition

Recognizing the whole rhythm of a Morse character instead of counting dots and dashes one by one. This is one of the most important long-term learning shifts.

On-air and operating terms

CQ

A general call that means you are calling any station that wants to answer. Beginners often meet it before their first real contact.

QSO

A contact or conversation between stations. On this site, first-QSO practice usually means preparing for a simple, realistic Morse exchange.

DE

Used between the called station and the calling station, roughly meaning “from.” It appears in many basic contact examples.

RST

A signal report shorthand covering readability, signal strength, and tone. Learners often see it in beginner operating guides.

QTH

A shorthand term used for location in radio exchanges. It appears often in beginner QSO vocabulary lists.

QRM

Interference from other signals. Learners may see this when reading about weak-signal conditions or real-world copying difficulty.

QSB

Signal fading over time. It matters because Morse that sounds clear at one moment may become harder to copy a few seconds later.

73

A traditional closing meaning best regards. It shows up frequently in amateur radio operating language.

Equipment and sending terms

Straight key

A basic hand key where you press down for each element. It is historically important and often used to explain early Morse sending.

Paddle

A lever-style input device usually used with an electronic keyer. It helps produce more consistent sending than manual straight-key timing for many operators.

Keyer

An electronic device or function that turns paddle movement into timed Morse elements. It reduces some of the timing burden on the sender.

Sidetone

The tone you hear locally while sending. It helps the operator monitor rhythm and timing even before the signal reaches another station.

Spacing

The gaps between elements, characters, and words. Poor spacing can make even correctly sent dots and dashes much harder to understand.

Bug

A semi-automatic mechanical key used by some operators. It can send repeated dashes or dots in a characteristic style and usually requires practice.

Learning and review terms that matter

Plateau

A period where speed or accuracy stops improving for a while. Plateaus are common and usually call for better review structure, not panic.

Error review

The process of looking at repeated misses, slow patterns, or unstable character groups and turning them into a focused practice decision.

Consistency

Short, repeatable practice over time. In Morse learning, steady repetition usually matters more than occasional marathon sessions.

Effective difficulty

How hard a practice session really feels once speed, spacing, character mix, fatigue, and device type are all taken together.
